🗄️ Schloss — what the balance sheet says

Data: {{ data_source }}

Walter Schloss ran 1955–2002 at roughly 21% gross, about 15.3% net to his partners, against ~10% for the index. He did it with no research staff, no company visits and no forecasts — just the annual report. This screen runs the half of his method that filings alone can answer: tangible book, net current assets, debt, how long it has survived, whether it pays a dividend, and whether management is quietly paying itself in stock.

“I like the idea of company-paid dividends, because I think it makes management a little more aware of stockholders… I don't like debt. I've seen too many companies get into trouble with it.”

“You used to be able to tell when the market was too high by the fact that the working capital stocks disappeared.” — the one piece of market timing he allowed himself, and a measurement rather than a forecast. The net-net count is the reading that matters, and it is the one that needs a price.

The stricter version of the same question — Riklis, 1949

A finance professor at Ohio State set his class an odd assignment: find a company holding more cash than its entire stock was worth in the market. Meshulam Riklis found dozens — in cement, whole companies cost less than the money in their own bank accounts — and spent the next forty years running variations on that homework problem. The Riklis column is that test: (cash + receivables − all liabilities) ÷ market cap. Above 1.00 the liquid assets alone, after settling every claim, exceed what the equity costs.

It is stricter than the net-net beside it. Graham counts every current asset, so a warehouse of unsold inventory carries the same weight as money in the bank. A purchase price does not get recovered out of inventory, so this counts only the two lines whose book value is their value. Every liability is charged, not just current ones — cash pledged against debt recovers nothing, and charging current liabilities alone would describe a company nobody could actually buy.

What it leaves out is what he cared most about. His numerator also counted salable real estate and surplus divisions. Property is filed at historical cost less depreciation — land bought in 1960 sits on the balance sheet at 1960 prices — and that gap was the edge. A version of this reading property off the balance sheet would understate the very thing he was hunting while looking more thorough, so it is excluded and named rather than approximated. Divisions need segment data and a judgement about which are surplus. This is the floor his test would have cleared, not his test.

And it is one rule of five. The other four were an acquirer's toolkit: pay in seller notes and convertible preferred rather than cash, factor the receivables and sell the real estate before the next deal needs the money, never pay cash for a company that cannot fund the next purchase, and shape the terms around what the seller actually wants. Every one of them requires owning the company. A minority buyer gets the margin of safety and none of the machinery — which is why this is a column here and not a strategy.

How this differs from the other screens, and what it deliberately does not do
  • No quality gates. Compounders requires ROIC, revenue consistency and cash conversion. Schloss ignored all three — he was buying the assets, not the engine, and many of his holdings would fail every quality test here. The two screens must never be blended; the average of them is a portfolio neither approach would hold.
  • Financials are kept. Compounders drops SIC 6000–6799 because ROIC and free cash flow are meaningless for a bank. Book value is exactly the right lens for one, so they stay.
  • Survival is measured from the filing index, not the financials. XBRL starts around 2010, so no company can show more than ~16 years of machine-readable data — measuring history from it would put every company below his 20-year bar. EDGAR's quarterly indexes reach back to 1994 and answer the question directly.
  • Missing is never a pass, and never a fail either. A “?” means the filing did not carry the concept. Neither debt tag filed does not mean no debt — the build only clears that gate when the entire liability stack is small enough to prove it.
  • What is still missing: insider ownership percentage (it lives in DEF 14A proxies as prose, not XBRL), and the whole price half. Insider alignment — “managements that own a lot of stock” — is scored as unknown for every row today rather than assumed.
