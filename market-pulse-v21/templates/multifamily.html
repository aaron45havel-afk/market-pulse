How the board decides

Filters are yes/no. A ZIP meets every one you set or it is off the board, and the box above the table says how many each removed. A ZIP with no figure for a filter is removed too — and counted separately, because a missing number is a gap in our data, not a finding about the place. It never passes by default.

Score ranks only the ZIPs that survived your filters, so it means best of the ones you'd consider, not best in the state. Each priority is a percentile among those ZIPs, blended by your weights:

  • Cash flow — your monthly cost after rent from the other units. Lower is better.
  • Rental yield — cap rate at measured Zillow rents.
  • Price growth — the ZIP's own home values over the last 36 months, annualised. Measured, not a forecast.
  • Neighborhood income — median household income (Census).
  • Educated neighbors — share of adults with a bachelor's degree (Census).

A row missing one signal is scored on the rest and marked, rather than dropped or scored as zero. Rows whose safety nobody measured, when you choose to show them, are listed after every verified row regardless of score.

Weather is NOAA's 1991–2020 normals — thirty-year averages — from the nearest weather station that actually measures each thing, within 25 miles. Temperatures and snow are matched separately, because most stations only measure rain and snow. Stations whose figures NOAA estimated from their neighbours are skipped. Hover a Climate cell to see how far away the station is. A hill town matched to a valley station will read a little warm.{% if climate_meta %} ({{ '{:,}'.format(climate_meta.stations.temperature) }} temperature stations; figures as of {{ climate_meta.as_of }}.){% endif %}

Natural hazards are FEMA's National Risk Index — but not its headline risk ratings. Those multiply expected damage by the local population's social vulnerability, so the same flood rates "riskier" in a poorer area, and they are relative: a wildfire score of 64 is labelled "Very Low". This board uses FEMA's expected damage a year to buildings, in dollars per $100k of building value, spread from census tracts to ZIPs by land area. It shows dollars, not national ranks, on purpose: most ZIPs expect almost no wildfire or earthquake damage, so a rank would call $1 a year "worse than 80% of the country". {% if hz_median.total is defined and hz_median.total is not none %}The typical U.S. ZIP expects about {{ usd(hz_median.total) }} a year per $100k from all four together. {% endif %} The Hazards column adds the four up — amber from $250, red from $500, with the biggest of the four named. Hover a cell to see it on the row's purchase price — an overstatement, because the price includes land and FEMA counts only the building. It is FEMA's model, and it can surprise: levee-protected central New Orleans (70112) expects about $27 a year of flood damage per $100k but $337 from hurricanes, and FEMA's inland-flood estimate sets a baseline almost everywhere — downtown Phoenix (85004, $148) comes out above Miami Beach (33139, $88). Near a coast, read flood and hurricane together; the Hazards column already does. It is an area average; a specific building's flood zone matters more than any ZIP figure, so check it on FEMA's flood maps before you buy. Connecticut has no hazard figures: FEMA's tracts use the state's 2022 renumbering and the Census ZIP-to-tract file doesn't.{% if hazards_meta %} (NRI {{ hazards_meta.nri_version }}; built {{ hazards_meta.as_of }}.){% endif %}

All states. The national board prices every ZIP with its own state's tax and insurance rates, safety record and structural flags, so a ZIP's row is identical to its row on its own state's board — only its score differs, because scores are relative to the other ZIPs on the board.

What isn't here, and why. The database has columns called walk_score, restaurant_score and crime_index. None is a measurement: the first two are curves on population density and the third is density + income + education. Density is offered once, honestly, as Area type; the other two are not offered, because filtering on them would be filtering on density three times.

House-hack math: purchase = min(local single-family median × the {{ units }}-unit price factor, your cap). A 2–4 unit does not cost the single-family median, so every row is adjusted before anything is underwritten, and insurance carries a multi-unit uplift over the SFR premium. Those factors are conservative defaults, not measurements — 2–4 unit ask prices aren't in this dataset. PITI = P&I + state-level property tax + state-level insurance + FHA MIP (0.55% annual, life-of-loan when down < 10%). Expected rent = (units − 1) × local median rent. Net monthly cost = PITI − expected rent, before utilities, maintenance, capex and vacancy. Negative = you live free + cash flow.

FHA self-sufficiency (3- and 4-unit only): FHA requires 75% × total rents ≥ PITI for the loan to fund. Two-unit purchases are exempt from this rule. We mark FHA on rows that pass, FHA ✗ on rows that fail at your input price.

  • 3-yr trend glyphs: ▲ accelerating · → steady · ⚠ decelerating · ▼ declining.
  • Trajectory veto — a declining ZIP's score is capped (at 79/69/59 depending on how many state-level structural flags are active); a decelerating ZIP loses 5 points. Vetoed rows show capped.
  • Durable cap — gross yield × 92% (vacancy) − state property tax − state insurance ÷ home value − 1.5% maintenance reserve.
  • Stressed — house-hack net if rents come in 10% light, insurance reprices +30%, and you eat one vacant month a year.
