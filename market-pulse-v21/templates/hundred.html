How long this page has actually held anything

Both source books say buy right, hold on — Mayer's dataset needed 15 to 45 years. A screen that rewrites itself every month is not implementing that, and the honest way to find out is to count. The sister Lynch board passed 10 names in June, 11 in July and 4 in August, and exactly one survived July into August; most of that turnover was a rules change rather than a market.

How many bets this really is

A long list is not a diversified one. If most of these names sell into the same end-market, a basket built from them is one bet wearing many tickers — and that is invisible from any other number on the page. Effective sectors is 1 / HHI over the listed set's SIC divisions.

What the screen actually looked at

Coverage — how often each criterion could be measured at all. This is the number the page is built around: six of the thirteen can never be scored, so the best any company reaches is seven.

What this screen deliberately does not do
  • It does not score six of the thirteen, ever. Not for any company, not in any month. Those cells are hatched, not blank — a hatched cell means the data does not exist, which is a different fact from a company that did not file a tag.
  • Every proxy for them was tested and broke. Capital allocation proxies put Intel FY2015–19 in the top band. Moat proxies scored Tupperware "Great" unanimously until it filed Chapter 11. Pay-based incentive proxies read Super Micro's $1 CEO salary as perfect alignment. A proxy that needs a paragraph of caveats will be read without the paragraph.
  • There is no composite score, and no percentage. A score divided by "the criteria we happened to measure" ranks companies by how little is known about them. The headline is a fraction with both numbers showing — 4 of the 6 measured, of 13 — because "67%" is the same figure for a company seen clearly and one barely seen at all.
  • Three criteria can only say no. Capital allocation, incentives and moat are the checklist's veto criteria, and no free source can establish any of them positively — every proxy tested for that was broken by a real company. What filings can sometimes show is the negative: margin holding while revenue falls is a harvest, not a moat; capital going in while incremental returns fall is destruction. So they veto and never vote. A count-of-passes rule could not have used them at all: adding them as scoreable criteria would have taken this board from 148 names to roughly 468, which is the opposite of what the criteria are for.
  • "Quiet" is not a pass. A veto cell that is not red means nothing visible is wrong. It is not evidence that anything is right, and there is deliberately no positive band.
  • The size gate does not also vote. Criterion 13 used to gate membership and count as a pass, so 99 of the 111 companies sitting at the four-pass minimum were counting "it is small" as one of their four — clearing three quality gates, not four. It still shows its band in the grid; it no longer votes, and the list is honest at 49 rather than flattering at 148.
  • Criterion 13 is a precondition, not a quality. The other twelve describe how good a business is; this one describes whether a hundred-bagger is arithmetically available from here. The first run listed NVIDIA at $5.2 trillion — a hundred-bagger from there is $520tn, several times world GDP — because it cleared four other criteria while 13 sat marked Yikes. That was the right band and the wrong consequence, so the checklist's own Yikes boundary is now a gate. The source says it plainly: "Is Microsoft a great company? Yes. Can it become a 100-bagger from now on? Probably not."
  • A P/E above 500x is a near-zero denominator, not a valuation. Arista arrived with a multiple of 69,913,371 against $245.5bn of market cap — about $3,500 of net income — and printed a peer discount of −198,956,563%. The low end was already bounded at 3x for the same reason; the high end never needed it until this page had no downstream gate to catch it. It is withheld rather than clamped, and kept out of the peer median so it moves nobody else's row.
  • Criterion 9 is the one that hurts most. Analyst coverage is inverted — zero analysts is the best score — and it is licensed terminal data. Losing it removes the thesis's own explanation of why a quality company would ever be cheap.
  • It cannot see what delisted. The universe is current SEC registrants; a company that failed has no ticker and is not in the source file at all. Both source books have the same bias — they read traits backwards off companies that already returned 100x — and neither this page nor those books can tell you the base rate.
  • It is not a buy list. Six criteria are yours to answer by reading a proxy statement and an annual report. The page's job is to make that list short.
